What this page groups together

The source text points to initiation rites, guild greetings, passwords, gestures, and question-and-answer rituals as social techniques for proving that someone belongs, knows, or has trained without fully disclosing the protected material itself.

These are not formal cryptographic protocols. But they are recognizably proof practices, and they matter because they show that the problem of limited disclosure is older than modern mathematics.

Guilds as a strong example

The guild example is especially useful for HyperDoc because it combines competence, secrecy, mobility, and institutional gatekeeping. A journeyman arriving in a new town needed to demonstrate enough knowledge to be accepted without surrendering the techniques that remained part of a master's craft. Knowledge was shown through effects, responses, and participation in a ritual frame.

Relation to existing HyperDoc work

This page strengthens a theme already present in Digital Identity and Social Engineering: proof systems are never only technical. They operate through visible rituals, expectations, authority, and learned responses.

That makes the historical examples useful for two opposite reasons. They show how communities recognized legitimate members before modern technical proof systems. They also show how every proof ritual creates a social attack surface, because legitimacy is performed in front of other people.

Why not collapse this into social engineering

Social authentication is not the same thing as social engineering. The first names legitimate recognition practices; the second names exploitative misuse of trust and ritual. Keeping the concepts separate makes later analysis more precise.
