Concepts, DMX Topics, and Topic Maps

hyperdoc

This page gives an operational definition of a concept and relates it to DMX topics and topic maps.

The weaker starting point here is not yet a fully stabilized subject. The weaker starting point is a distinction that can be marked and revisited, plus a boundary that keeps it from collapsing into neighboring distinctions.

The strongest topic-map reading is: everything is a topic. That does not mean every topic is equally important. It means every identifiable distinction can be treated as a topic candidate, and some of those candidates later become subjects with identity, names, occurrences, and associations.

Operational definition of concept

A concept begins as a distinction the system can mark and revisit, independent of any one rendering, file, page, or implementation shortcut.

Operationally, a concept should have at least:

  • distinguishability: this is not that
  • a boundary that keeps the distinction from collapsing into adjacent ones
  • a re-addressable marker, handle, or name that lets the distinction be revisited

Subject identity is a stronger later commitment. It becomes relevant when the system needs to preserve sameness across occurrences, names, contexts, or relations.

That means a concept is not merely a string and not merely a page title. It may begin as a distinction marker, and only later become the identifiable subject that different pages, views, and objects may refer to as the same thing.

In topic-map terms, this is exactly the point of saying that everything is a topic: persons, pages, tools, reports, workflows, and even relations can all first appear as distinctions worth marking, and some of them then mature into identifiable subjects instead of being reduced to document fragments.

The key strengthening is this: associations are also topics. An association is not merely an edge drawn between two already-real topics. It can itself be a first-class subject with identity, names, occurrences, and further associations.

Operational definition of subject identity

Subject identity is the rule that lets a system decide when two references are about the same subject even when they appear under different names, pages, views, or representations.

This is not the minimum starting point. It is the stronger rule you add once the system needs to preserve sameness across multiple occurrences of the same distinction.

Operationally, a subject has identity if it can remain the same subject across:

  • different names
  • different documents
  • different views
  • different relations
  • different moments in time

So subject identity is not the current page title or one label string. It is the stable reference point that multiple names, occurrences, and associations point to.

For the larger question of preserving sameness when names, pages, views, and associations vary, see Preserving Sameness Across Representations.

Lisp object for concept

In Lisp terms, that means a concept object should be able to represent both:

a distinction/boundary stage, and a stronger subject-identity stage when required.

The object model on this page is therefore easier to read if you treat identity as one possible strengthening of a concept, not as the minimum condition for calling something a concept at all.

Relation to topic maps

Topic maps make this explicit. A topic map is not only a collection of pages. It is a graph of topics, where each topic represents a subject and can have:

  • names
  • occurrences
  • associations

In the stronger reading relevant here, an association is not only a link field inside another topic. It can itself be treated as a topic whenever the relation has its own meaning and needs to be inspected, named, or related to other things.

This is useful here because it separates the subject from any one document or user interface. That is close to the distinction already needed in HyperDoc: the concept is not identical to the page that documents it.

The phrase "everything is a topic" is therefore not inflationary rhetoric. It means the system can mark distinctions and boundaries first, and only then decide where stronger subject identity is worth preserving. Page/document structure still comes later.

Relation to DMX topics

A DMX topic can be read as a concrete runtime realization of the stronger stage of the same idea. It is an addressable object that stands for one subject and carries type, values, and associations.

In that sense, a DMX topic is not just inspired by topic maps. It is a direct operationalization of the topic-map idea once a distinction has been promoted into an identifiable subject rather than left as only a page fragment or string label.

Why this matters for ownership and architecture

The question "which layer owns the concept?" becomes clearer once a concept is treated as a subject with stable identity rather than as a paragraph in a page.

A layer owns a concept when it defines that subject's meaning, rules, and lifecycle. Other layers may host it or document it, but they do not thereby become its owner.

Pragmatic conclusion

A concept should first be treatable as an inspectable distinction with a boundary. Topic maps and DMX topics then provide a useful stronger model whenever identity, occurrences, and associations need to be made explicit instead of left implicit in page structure.

Put more strongly: if everything important can be treated as a topic, then the architecture can stay oriented around subjects and relations rather than around whichever page or package happened to mention them first.

That includes relations themselves. Once associations are also topics, the system can talk about relations in the same explicit way that it talks about entities, pages, tools, and workflows.
