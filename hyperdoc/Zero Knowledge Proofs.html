Zero Knowledge Proofs

hyperdoc

Zero Knowledge Proofs are cryptographic proof systems that let one party prove possession of some knowledge without revealing that knowledge itself. This page starts a HyperDoc entry point for the concept and relates the topic to an already existing HyperDoc cluster about digital identity, trust, abstraction, fraud, and public-system design.

Why add this page now

The current HyperDoc repository already contains a strong critical vocabulary for digital identity systems. It distinguishes abstraction, over-identifying infrastructures, social engineering, trust erosion, biometrics, and public design constraints. What it does not yet contain is a durable concept page for zero knowledge proofs themselves or for their deeper social prehistory.

The immediate value of adding this page is therefore not to pretend that the mathematics is already covered in full. The value is to give the existing identity-and-trust cluster a new bridge to a technical tradition that tries to solve a very old human problem: proving enough without disclosing too much.

Working definition

Zero knowledge proofs matter here because they change the architecture of trust. Instead of proving identity or entitlement by revealing the secret, the record, or the whole person, they try to prove only the relevant claim. In HyperDoc terms, that makes them especially relevant to pages already concerned with over-identification, identity creep, and the mismatch between what a task needs and what an identity infrastructure demands.

Relation to the existing HyperDoc scholarship

  • Digital Identity as Abstraction: zero knowledge proofs resist the reduction of a person to an overexposed bundle of machine-readable attributes by letting systems ask for narrower proofs.
  • Over-Identification and Identity Creep: zero knowledge proofs are relevant wherever a system currently asks for more identity material than the task strictly requires.
  • Digital Identity and Social Engineering: zero knowledge proofs do not eliminate human manipulation, but they sharpen the boundary between a mathematically valid proof and a socially coerced or misleading request.
  • Trust Erosion from Identity Fraud: better proof systems can matter because identity failures degrade both human trust and institutional trust.
  • Design Constraints for Public Digital Identity: if public identity systems adopt privacy-preserving proofs, they should still preserve human correction paths and avoid turning technical elegance into new coercive defaults.

Historical scope of this starter slice

This starter slice begins with the first page of History of Zero Knowledge Proofs, which emphasizes deep social precursors rather than twentieth-century mathematics. That means the first durable neighbors are not Shannon, RSA, or interactive proof systems yet. They are guild ritual, confession, social vouching, and the problem of identity before documents.

The mathematical history should be added next as a separate continuation cluster once the social and institutional bridge is in place.

Editorial boundary

This page does not claim that older ritual, confessional, or village practices were literally zero knowledge proofs in the formal cryptographic sense. It claims something narrower: they are historically useful precursors for the same recurring design question of trust without unnecessary disclosure.
