Core question

The durable question is simple: how can one prove what matters without disclosing everything one knows, everything one is, or everything a system could collect?

The first page of History of Zero Knowledge Proofs treats that question as much older than cryptography. Long before formal mathematics, human institutions had to decide how to recognize membership, competence, conscience, or identity without making every relevant secret public.

Why this matters in the current HyperDoc cluster

The existing digital-identity pages already argue that modern systems often ask for too much. They reduce persons to abstractions, normalize wider control surfaces, and make fraud or coercion socially expensive. Trust without disclosure is the missing positive counter-question: what would a better proof relation look like?

Relation to existing pages

  • Digital Identity as Abstraction: trust without disclosure pushes against systems that know persons only by flattening them into serializable fields.
  • Over-Identification and Identity Creep: it offers a way to ask for less and therefore to build smaller identity surfaces.
  • Trust Erosion from Identity Fraud: it matters because bad proof relations degrade social trust and institutional trust.
  • Design Constraints for Public Digital Identity: it names one family of constraints that public systems should increasingly satisfy.

Negative and positive readings

In a negative reading, trust without disclosure is a criticism of systems that demand excessive proof. In a positive reading, it is a design ideal for institutions that want narrower, more proportional, and less extractive proof relations.

Editorial boundary

This page does not say that every privacy-preserving design is a zero knowledge proof, and it does not say that formal proofs alone solve the human problems of trust. It marks the narrower bridge: privacy-preserving proof techniques become socially and politically relevant where identity systems are otherwise tempted to over-collect.
