Inspectable review contracts for automated code review

This page materializes one HyperDoc answer to the question of how automated code-review criteria should be made explicit, modifiable, and reviewable rather than hidden in opaque prompts or unstated preferences.

Problem

Automated review is useful only if its criteria are visible and modifiable. Semantic equivalence analysis can be valuable, but normative critique always depends on declared assumptions about what matters, what counts as evidence, and what the reviewer is allowed to reject. If those assumptions stay hidden, the review becomes hard to contest, tune, or compare with later reviews.

Review contract

A review contract is the durable object that carries the declared review criteria for one review task. In HyperDoc terms, it should make the following things explicit and inspectable:

  • priorities
  • priors
  • comparison mode
  • burden of proof
  • rejection policy
  • output shape

This is the boundary between useful analysis and opaque judgment. Without the contract, a review can still say what it prefers, but it cannot show why those preferences were authoritative for this task.

Priorities and priors

Review priorities state what the review optimizes for. They answer questions such as: should clarity outrank micro-optimization, should local convention fit outrank generic elegance, and how much weight should inspectability carry?

Review priors state what the review assumes before reading the code. They answer questions such as: is one implementation treated as a trusted reference, is the candidate assumed to be a student attempt, and is the task local-convention-sensitive or not?

Both are needed. Priorities without priors leave the task relation vague. Priors without priorities leave the evaluation target vague. A review that claims to be explicit must declare both.

Comparison modes

Comparison mode states what kind of review task is actually being run. These modes should not be conflated:

  • symmetric comparison: compare alternatives on the same footing
  • asymmetric exemplar critique: critique a candidate against a trusted exemplar
  • pedagogical review: optimize for learning and explanation, not only selection
  • migration/regression review: compare candidate behavior against an existing local contract or baseline

Each mode changes what counts as relevant evidence and what counts as a fair verdict. A reviewer that mixes these modes without naming them hides its own task definition.

Teacher/student exemplar

The student/teacher case is an asymmetric exemplar review, not a symmetric comparison. The point is not “which version is better in general?” but “how does the student candidate differ from the trusted teacher exemplar under the declared contract?”

In that mode, the priors matter directly: the student candidate and teacher exemplar do not carry the same burden. The trusted reference prior licenses a critique that asks where the candidate diverges, what trade-offs the divergence introduces, and whether those trade-offs are acceptable under the declared priorities. 

(:review-contract
  :priorities ((:clarity 1.0)
               (:inspectability 0.9)
               (:local-convention-fit 0.7)
               (:micro-optimization 0.2))
  :priors ((:candidate-role . :student)
           (:reference-role . :teacher)
           (:reference-trust . 0.85))
  :comparison-mode :asymmetric-exemplar
  :burden-of-proof (:show-tradeoffs :name-assumptions :cite-local-patterns)
  :rejection-policy :allowed
  :output-shape (:applied-priors
                 :applied-priorities
                 :alternatives
                 :tradeoffs
                 :verdict
                 :rejections
                 :uncertainty))

Required reviewer output shape

A reviewer operating under an inspectable contract should make its output shape explicit. At minimum, the review should list:

  • applied priorities
  • applied priors
  • options considered
  • trade-offs
  • verdict
  • rejected alternatives and why
  • uncertainty or missing evidence

This makes the trade-offs and rejections inspectable later. It also makes it possible to say that the review was coherent under one contract while still changing the contract for a different task.

Why this belongs in HyperDoc

HyperDoc is the right place for this answer because review criteria are part of the local documentation substrate, not just transient chat style. A review contract should be durable, inspectable, and linkable so later readers can see what assumptions were applied, which modes were chosen, and how a verdict was justified.

Leaving this only as an opaque prompt or chat-side norm would hide exactly the thing that the review is supposed to make explicit.
