Small Web Discovery and Aggregation

hyperdoc

Kevin Boone's article gives the cluster a concrete operational question: if the small web is really small, can its new material be aggregated in one place the way Gemini feed aggregators often do? The answer in the supplied material is structurally interesting: yes at machine scale, but not cleanly at reader scale.

Aggregation is limited by readers more than crawlers

The article's most reusable insight is that compute is not necessarily the hardest constraint. Once a sufficiently large feed list exists, crawling, timestamp extraction, and chronological ordering are straightforward enough. The actual bottleneck is that a daily list large enough to be representative quickly exceeds what a human can realistically read.

That makes “small web” aggregation unlike the romantic picture of one calm shared bulletin board. Distribution still matters, and selectivity remains necessary even in a non-commercial ecology.

Why Gemini feels different

Gemini aggregation appears manageable not because feeds are conceptually different there, but because the participating universe is smaller and more explicitly bounded. Once browser-based personal publishing is included, the discovered corpus becomes much larger and more unevenly active.

Opt-in as a readability strategy

Boone's update points toward a more practical pattern: opt-in aggregators, topic-specific aggregators, or other deliberately partial lenses. These do not solve the whole discovery problem, but they restore a workable relation between collection size and reading attention.

Implication for HyperDoc

HyperDoc should preserve the distinction between complete inventory and useful reading surface. A cluster can acknowledge scale without promising total daily legibility. That same distinction already appears elsewhere in HyperDoc when a complete graph exists but only some curated paths should be exposed as a first reading surface.
