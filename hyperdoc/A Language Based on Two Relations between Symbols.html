A Language Based on Two Relations between Symbols

hyperdoc

A Language Based on Two Relations between Symbols is the paper-level anchor for Agustin Rafael Martinez's 2022 Onward! '22 paper on Representar (pages 95-111, DOI 10.1145/3563835.3567660). The paper presents a language organized around two relations between symbols, substitution and categorization, and argues that the same language can span both visual and textual programming surfaces.

For HyperDoc, this matters as a neighboring reference system rather than as a blueprint. The paper keeps several questions close together without collapsing them: language as a cueing medium instead of mere label storage, inspectable execution through substitution traces, continuity between visual and textual authoring, and the possibility of domain-specific notations inside one environment.

The attached supplement is useful here as concrete supporting evidence, not as an import mandate. It shows text loadouts and models for exceptions and testing, natural and fractional numbers, arithmetic relations, and category-by-construction examples. It also shows how the language adapts its notation to mathematics within the same environment.

What the paper claims

The paper's direct claim is that symbolic language can be organized around two relations: substitution and categorization. Those relations are not presented merely as convenient notation. They are presented as the constitutive core of the language and as the basis for both its visual and textual forms.

The paper's medium claim is equally important. Representar is shown through a visual environment where user-defined drawings become symbols, through a textual environment with the same language, through a substitution browser that exposes execution steps, and through REPL-like experimentation where expressions can be tried and re-evaluated as the program changes.

Why it matters to HyperDoc

HyperDoc already cares about how a symbol or phrase can act as a route into a richer semantic surface rather than as a dead label. Martínez's paper does not prove HyperDoc's retrieval-language view, but it does provide a nearby design argument in which symbols are treated as operational cues inside an inspectable transformation system.

The paper also matters because it keeps execution inspectable and keeps the visual and textual surfaces continuous. That is relevant to HyperDoc's own interest in inspectable semantic transition, documentation surfaces that stay close to runtime evidence, and approachable but still structured programming forms.

What seems reusable for HyperDoc

  • Language can be treated as a retrieval or cueing medium, not only as a set of names.
  • Execution can be exposed as a browsable sequence of substitutions instead of only as final output.
  • Visual and textual authoring can remain continuous without forcing an immediate language change.
  • One host environment can carry multiple domain-specific notations without pretending they are separate runtimes.
  • Approachability does not have to mean giving up structure, inspectability, or explicit transformation rules.

What should remain paper-specific

  • The stronger theoretical claim that these two relations are the fundamental basis of symbolic language should stay attributed to the paper.
  • The named Representar tools, editor layout, and substitution-priority details should remain reference-system specifics unless HyperDoc adopts them explicitly later.
  • The teaching scenarios and demos such as forest fires, robot behavior, booleans, and number systems remain examples on this page rather than new HyperDoc topic objects.
  • The supplement's concrete exception, testing, arithmetic, and number models are evidence of expressive range, not separate HyperDoc concept pages in this slice.

Boundary

HyperDoc is not implementing Representar here. This slice documents the paper, its supplement, and the conceptual neighborhood they open up for HyperDoc. The paper is treated as a reference system, a neighboring design argument, and a source of reusable distinctions. It is not silently promoted into HyperDoc's runtime, theory, or canonical architecture.
