Digital Identity and Social Engineering

hyperdoc

The article reports that major harms in digital identity often occur on the human side rather than through broken cryptography. HyperDoc uses this page to separate two questions that are too often collapsed: whether a system is technically well-designed, and whether its users can still be manipulated into authorising, disclosing, or trusting the wrong thing.

Why this matters

The article reports Diehm's claim that a fraudster does not need to defeat a system mathematically. A fraudster often only needs the victim to cooperate at the right moment, or to believe that the request is normal, urgent, or authoritative.

Attributed anchors

  • Swedish Bank-ID fraud is the clearest anchor in the Infosperber article: the article reports large losses caused by callers who pressured people into logging in and disclosing credentials.
  • The article explicitly says no encryption software needed to be hacked in that example.
  • The New Design Congress note Self-(De)termination adds a related anchor: deepfake calls and familiar faces can exploit trust even when the interface looks authentic.

Human weakness is not the same as user blame

The article's framing is not merely that users should be more careful. It implies that identity systems are being deployed into already vulnerable human settings, where urgency, shame, fear, trust, and institutional asymmetry are part of the attack surface.
