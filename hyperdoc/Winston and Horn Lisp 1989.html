Why keep a dedicated page

The book matters for three reasons that are still legible in HyperDoc: symbol manipulation as a first-class programming subject, a large collection of classic AI/Lisp worked examples, and an early CLOS-era framing that makes classes, generic functions, inheritance, and class precedence part of the same teaching arc rather than a separate specialist topic.

Three-part structure

Part One builds from Understanding Symbol Manipulation through Rules for Good Programming and Tools for Debugging. It covers Lisp basics, procedure definition, recursion, data abstraction, mapping, iteration, I/O, and debugging as the initial working toolkit.

Part Two moves into later-language mechanisms through Properties and Arrays, Macros and Backquote, Structures, Classes and Generic Functions, Lexical Variables, Generators, and Encapsulation, Special Variables, List Storage, Surgery, and Reclamation, and Lisp in Lisp. This is where the book becomes especially useful as background on CLOS, lexical closures, dynamic variables, storage discipline, and interpreter structure.

Part Three turns that toolkit toward larger worked examples such as Examples Involving Search, Constraint Propagation, Symbolic Pattern Matching, Streams and Delayed Evaluation, Backward Chaining and PROLOG, and Procedure-Writing Programs and Database Interfaces. The appendix The Computation of the Class Precedence List closes the book with explicit object-system method-order mechanics.

Reusable topics promoted from the book

HyperDoc promotes a selective second pass of reusable concept topics rather than every exercise or subsection. The highest-value carry-forward concepts here are Procedure abstraction, Data abstraction, Mapping primitive, Lexical closure, Special variable, Garbage collection, Forward chaining, Backward chaining, Natural-language interface, and Class precedence list.
