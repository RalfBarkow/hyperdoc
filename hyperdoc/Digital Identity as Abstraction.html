Digital Identity as Abstraction

hyperdoc

The article reports that Cade Diehm treats digital identity as an abstraction: a person is reduced to machine-readable parameters, criteria, and traces that a system can process. This is a durable critical concept for HyperDoc because it explains why a system can appear precise while still remaining brittle, coercive, or misleading.

Reduction to system-readable criteria

The article reports Diehm's claim that digital identity narrows people into a system's chosen attributes, identifiers, behavioural traces, and proofs. In that model, the important question is no longer whether a system knows a person in their full social complexity, but whether the submitted parameters satisfy the machine's acceptance criteria.

Why attackers do not need full personhood

This is why the article treats digital identity as structurally risky. An attacker does not need to imitate a whole person. The study claims that an attacker only needs to satisfy the relevant system criteria for authentication, authorisation, or risk scoring. That distinction helps explain why fraud and coercion can succeed even when a digital identity system appears technically sophisticated.

Boundary

HyperDoc uses this as a critical lens, not a total definition of all identity work. The point is not that every digital identity system is identical, but that the article reports a recurrent structural risk whenever human identity is compressed into a narrow technical representation and then treated as sufficient.
