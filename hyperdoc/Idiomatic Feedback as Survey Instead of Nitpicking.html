Idiomatic Feedback as Survey Instead of Nitpicking

hyperdoc

This page turns one thread of Konrad Feedback on Communication Pages into an operational rule for idiomatic Lisp review: treat local idiomatic cleanups as real but small, and reserve stronger claims for patterns that recur across the codebase with inspectable context.

A micro-improvement can be real without being strategically important

Replacing a trivial wrapper such as (lambda (reference) (list reference)) with #'list is a real idiomatic improvement. It shortens the code and uses a standard function designator directly.

But that single rewrite is still only a micro-improvement. By itself, it does not tell us much about the codebase.

What makes an idiomatic issue survey-worthy

A survey-worthy idiomatic issue is one that recurs across files, views, or helper patterns. Examples include multiple ad hoc lambda wrappers, repeated function-designator inconsistencies, repeated one-armed if forms where when or unless would read more clearly, or duplicated local helper skeletons that suggest a missing abstraction.

That is why the useful unit is a survey rather than a nit. The stronger claim needs recurrence, context, and similarity, not only one isolated example.

The editorial layer sits above recurrence

Even when recurrence is real, the survey result is still only the inventory or cluster. A human editorial note explains what that recurrence may mean, where the interpretation applies, and whether a rewrite is optional, recommended, or explicitly not recommended.

A cluster records a recurring code shape. An editorial note records a human interpretation of that shape. The note may recommend refactoring, contextual caution, or explicit retention. A repeated form is not a defect merely because it recurs.

That note layer should support at least observation, interpretation, context boundary, refactoring note, and counterexample or exception. It is the layer that turns inventory into HyperDoc rather than leaving the survey as a raw tally.

Notes should attach primarily to clusters rather than to isolated occurrences. Individual examples help the reader see the pattern, but the editorial object is the recurring shape plus its bounded interpretation.

Konrad example as editorial note

Pattern: trivial lambda wrappers around existing function designators.

Survey finding: several occurrences of (lambda (x) (list x))-like wrappers or other single-call lambdas.

Editorial note example: "This cluster shows recurring trivial lambda wrappers around directly callable functions. In isolated cases this is only a micro-idiom issue and does not obstruct reading. Where such wrappers recur across nearby views, replacing them with direct function designators can improve regularity and reduce noise. Not every occurrence should be changed automatically; preserve local clarity."

Provenance of the judgment

Editorial judgments are authored, dated interpretations, not automatic properties of the corpus. That provenance matters because the same recurring shape can be interpreted differently by different readers or at different times.

Operational meaning of "improve Lisp idioms"

  1. Find recurring patterns across the corpus.
  2. Inspect concrete examples in context.
  3. Distinguish minor cleanups from structural repetition.
  4. Refactor only where readability or regularity actually improves.

Review stance

  • Survey before doctrine: start with the corpus, not a checklist.
  • Examples before verdicts: inspect real cases before naming a weakness.
  • Recurrence before refactoring: repeated shapes justify stronger action than one-off nits.
  • Context before prescription: enclosing definitions and nearby helpers matter.
