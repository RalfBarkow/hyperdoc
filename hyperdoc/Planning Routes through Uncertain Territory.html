Planning Routes through Uncertain Territory

hyperdoc

This page imports the core representational claim from Drew McDermott and Ernest Davis's 1984 Artificial Intelligence paper: route planning in partly known environments requires both topological structure and metric structure, and the metric structure must tolerate uncertainty rather than pretending that all locations are sharp points.

Source identity

Source: Drew McDermott and Ernest Davis, “Planning Routes through Uncertain Territory,” Artificial Intelligence, volume 22, number 2, 1984, pages 107–156.

Source-backed claims

  • A cognitive-map implementation should not collapse topology and metric geometry. The paper's design keeps topological assertions in an assertional database and metric information in a fuzzy map.
  • A fuzzy map records relative position, orientation, and scale in convenient frames of reference, with fuzzy coordinates represented as interval fuzzboxes rather than as fixed values.
  • The same spatial information also needs an inverted retrieval structure: a discrimination tree that can retrieve objects by location and other attributes instead of only answering questions about named objects.
  • Assimilation is not just append-only fact storage. A new spatial fact may tighten existing fuzzy coordinate ranges, or it may force remapping by rearranging or adding frames of reference.
  • Route planning is modeled as a staged process: first find the approximate direction and topological path, then refine the route around barriers and through conduits using the retrieval modules.

Local vocabulary imported into HyperDoc

Topological and metric split

HyperDoc should preserve the paper's split between qualitative graph-like relations and quantitative/metric constraints. A link, association, route, station, or containment claim is not the same thing as an exact coordinate. This is a useful boundary for documentation interfaces that make routes visible without pretending that every anchor is equally stable or equally precise.

Fuzzy map

A fuzzy map is not imported here as a finished implementation. It is imported as a design pattern: when an interface knows “roughly where” an object, station, pane, source span, or DOM anchor belongs, the uncertainty should be represented explicitly. For HyperDoc, this is relevant to source stations, DOM anchors, route annotations, topic placement, and any future route view that compares local and remote frames.

Frob tree and mquants

McDermott and Davis collapse frame and object into a hybrid called a frob. Adjacent frobs store memorized fuzzy quantities, or mquants, and other quantities are computed along frame paths. The HyperDoc lesson is that an inspectable object can also be a local reference frame: the same thing may be content, anchor, coordinate frame, and retrieval station, depending on the view.

Assimilation

The paper's assimilation vocabulary is especially important for HyperDoc. A newly imported source may merely sharpen an existing topic, but it may also require a local reorganization of neighboring topics, pages, anchors, and references. The two imported operations are:

  • fuzz constriction: tightening an existing range of possibilities, and
  • remapping: changing the frame structure so that a new fact becomes expressible.

Discrimination-tree retrieval

The discrimination tree is an inverted map for retrieval. It turns “given these spatial properties, what object is relevant?” into a first-class query. HyperDoc's corresponding use is not geometric search alone; it is source and topic retrieval by anchor context, relation kind, route role, traversability, and page neighborhood.

Barrier/conduit route planning

The route planner treats barriers and conduits as semantic spatial objects: walls block; doors, paths, and openings permit traversal for particular navigators; semiconduits may be traversable but still require planning. The paper distinguishes traversal barriers from penetration barriers, so the relevant question is not only whether an object blocks motion inside itself, but also whether the navigator can get through its boundary. In HyperDoc language, the useful import is that a route is not a line. A route is an inspectable plan through constraints, with semantic roles for blockers, openings, policies, and intermediate stations.

SPAM module crosswalk

Paper component Role in the paper HyperDoc import
TOPO Topological inferences over inclusion, overlap, parts, and barriers. Graph/relation reasoning over topics, pages, anchors, and route constraints.
MEASURE Metric queries over fuzzy maps, including term values and consistency tests. Explicit uncertainty around anchor stability, relative placement, and route evidence.
FIND Retrieval of objects from spatial properties using an inverted discrimination tree. Discovery of relevant pages, topics, anchors, and route objects from contextual properties.
Assimilator Constrains fuzz or remaps frames when new facts arrive. Source-station assimilation that can sharpen or reorganize the local topic/page model.
Navigator / route planner Finds direction and topology, then works around barriers and through conduits. Route-as-object planning and inspection rather than disposable gesture trails.

Boundary notes

  • This page does not claim that HyperDoc now implements the SPAM algorithms. It imports the paper's representational vocabulary and route-planning decomposition.
  • The paper itself excludes or defers execution, multiple-destination route planning, moving objects other than the navigator, vision integration, robust replanning, contradiction handling, familiar-route retrieval, and errand-running.
  • The paper's “fuzzy” intervals should not be read as a probabilistic model; the authors explicitly choose simple ranges to keep computation tractable under limited information.

HyperDoc editorial synthesis

This source belongs beside the existing Drew McDermott planning cluster, but it also speaks directly to HyperDoc's route vocabulary. It is an early example of route planning as an interaction between graph topology, uncertain metric evidence, retrieval indexes, and staged plan refinement. That is the useful bridge: HyperDoc routes should remain inspectable semantic objects whose evidence and uncertainty can be reviewed, not only visual connectors drawn between two rendered things.
