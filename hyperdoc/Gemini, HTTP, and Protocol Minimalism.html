Gemini appears as one deliberately constrained answer; ordinary browser-based publishing appears as another; and the HN thread adds disputes over JavaScript, cookies, TLS, Tor, plaintext HTTP, signatures, and what kind of simplification really matters.

Gemini as one answer, not the answer

Gemini is valued in the source material because its constraints reduce commercial pressure and keep implementation scope small. But the thread also shows why it should not be treated as the singular essence of the small web: some readers want ordinary browsers, richer media, or broader reach; others value precisely the narrowing Gemini imposes.

Minimalism can target different layers

One camp wants protocol-level simplicity. Another wants to keep HTTP and the browser but reject tracking, heavy client-side complexity, and opaque adtech. A third keeps the browser stack but argues that the decisive line is not JS versus no-JS, but surveillance and third-party control.

TLS and privacy are boundary questions, not pure doctrine

The discussion over rejecting encryption is best imported as a boundary topic, not as repository doctrine. It raises real tensions: maintenance overhead, microcontroller friendliness, authenticity, ISP meddling, passive surveillance, reader privacy, and the role of Tor. HyperDoc should preserve those tensions without endorsing plaintext publishing as a general ideal.

Implication for HyperDoc

Use this page to keep protocol choices near each other while making their boundaries explicit. “Gemini”, “no-tracking web”, and “browser-constrained small web” are adjacent, but they are not synonyms and should not share one flattened topic title.
