Editorial Control Surface and the Becoming of the Book

Editorial control surface is not a standard term of art in publishing or digital humanities. It is a useful synthesis for a particular kind of interface: one that does not merely display workflow status, nor merely archive provenance after the fact, but helps editors steer a work in progress while preserving an accountable record of how that work is taking shape.

In the Mech / HyperDoc setting, this means a surface that lets an editor inspect and compare bounded walks, generated drafts, exclusions, warnings, overlaps, and saved report pages while deciding what to try next. Such a surface does not only document a run. It participates in editorial judgment.

Why “control surface” is the right emphasis

A dashboard can remain passive. It can show tasks, counts, stages, or queue positions. A provenance graph can remain retrospective. It can explain how an artifact came to be. A scholarly edition can remain presentational. It can expose variants, facsimiles, and editorial principles without helping guide the next editorial move.

A control surface is stronger. It joins visibility and action. It lets one see what the system has done, what it is doing, and what should be done next. In this sense it is not simply a report layer on top of publication. It is part of the machinery by which publication is shaped.

Prior art: four converging traditions

1. Provenance and workflow systems

In W3C PROV, provenance is information about entities, activities, and agents involved in producing a thing. That is the right baseline for a Mech run record: pages and reports are entities, block executions are activities, and authors, editors, or automated procedures are agents.

Workflow systems such as VisTrails pushed this further by treating reproducibility and provenance-aware publication as first-class concerns. The interesting precedent is not merely that workflows can be replayed, but that the history of transformations becomes part of the published research object.

noWorkflow sharpened the lesson again: useful provenance should be capturable from ordinary scripts and ordinary working habits, not only from heavyweight workflow formalisms. For Mech, that supports a model in which script blocks remain author-facing text while still emitting a structured run record.

2. Moldable inspection and debugging

The Moldable Inspector and Moldable Debugger add a second crucial move. Preserving runtime evidence is not enough; one needs domain-specific views that answer the questions that actually arise in the current practice. Generic logs are too low-level. Generic debuggers leave a gap between domain questions and tool support.

For editorial work, that means views such as chronology, findings, boundary, overlap, source-to-artifact, and run-comparison are not luxuries. They are the actual working surfaces through which editorial reasoning becomes inspectable.

3. Editorial dashboards and workflow interfaces

Existing editorial systems already provide dashboard-like control surfaces. Platforms such as OMP or OJS expose tasks, workflow stages, action buttons, participants, queues, and editorial history. This is genuine prior art for the managerial side of editorial control.

But these systems usually control submissions and responsibilities rather than the internal becoming of a text. They help move a manuscript from submission to publication. They generally do not preserve the computational genesis of the assembled artifact in a form that can be inspected and compared as part of editorial reasoning.

4. Genetic criticism, scholarly editing, and book history

The strongest humanities-side prior art comes from genetic criticism and scholarly editing. Genetic criticism is concerned less with the final text than with reconstructing and analysing the writing process through drafts, notes, proofs, and related materials. In that tradition, the important object is not only the finished work but the avant-texte and the sequence of transformations that led toward it.

Digital scholarly editing extends this by building interfaces for variants, facsimiles, transcriptions, and comparisons. The Beckett Digital Manuscript Project is a clear example: its goal is to represent the evolutionary dynamics of composition, not just a stabilized text.

Book history and the sociology of texts widen the frame further. The material form of texts, and the editorial decisions that reshape them, are part of meaning-making rather than neutral packaging. Scholarly editing therefore does not merely reproduce a text; it mediates between versions, authorities, and interpretations.

Guides the becoming of the book

That phrase can now be made precise.

To say that a run ledger or editorial control surface guides the becoming of the book is to say that it serves three roles at once:

  • it preserves process evidence about how the current candidate book was assembled
  • it presents that evidence in editorially meaningful views
  • it supports next-step judgment about what the editor should try, accept, reject, or compare

In a story-neighborhood workflow, this means the surface can show, for example:

  • which gateway pages entered at one click
  • how two-click and three-click expansion changed the garden
  • where overlaps concentrate
  • which pages were excluded or downgraded and why
  • which draft and synopsis pages were emitted from which walk
  • how one run differs from an earlier timestamped run

At that point the ledger is no longer mere bookkeeping. It becomes laufende Buchführung in the strongest sense: an ongoing accounting that helps conduct the editorial process itself.

Why this matters for HyperDoc and Mech

Mech already has the architectural seam for this. A block execution already knows the authored command, normalized operation and arguments, body, runtime state, and emitted results. HyperDoc already values inspectable objects over opaque output.

What is still missing is the move from transient execution evidence to a durable, inspectable, comparable editorial object. Once that exists, the natural views are not only provenance views but editorial views: what changed in the candidate book, what stayed stable, what was lost, which boundary was applied, and which variation seems more faithful to the intended story.

In that form, an editorial control surface becomes the bridge between computational provenance and editorial practice. It preserves enough evidence to justify the draft, and enough structure to steer the next draft.

Sharper formulation

An editorial control surface is an inspectable interface that joins workflow state, provenance, editorial findings, and action paths so that the process of assembling a book can be both accounted for and steered.

Or, more briefly:

The editorial control surface does not just record book production; it helps steer the book’s becoming.
