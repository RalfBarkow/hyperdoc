## What the sources say (quotations)

**Plato, *Theaetetus* 201c–d (Fowler).** Theaetetus reports having heard someone
say "that knowledge was true opinion accompanied by reason". The Greek has
"μετὰ λόγου". This is the passage usually cited for knowledge as true belief
with an account; the dialogue goes on to examine it. Only this section was read.

**Ferrier 1854, Introduction § 55 (OCR).** "This section of the science is
properly termed the EPISTEMOLOGY — the doctrine or theory of knowing, just as
ontology is the doctrine or theory of being … It answers the general question,
'What is knowing and the known?' — or more shortly, 'What is knowledge?'"
§ 59 introduces a third section, "the agnoiology"; § 61: "1. Epistemology.
2. Agnoiology. 3. Ontology." That Ferrier *coined* the English word is stated by
Treccani ("termine coniato (1854) dal filosofo scozz. J. F. Ferrier"), a
dictionary; it was not checked against the OED. What was observed is the use in
1854, not priority.

**Stanford Encyclopedia (Steup and Neta).** "The term 'epistemology' comes from
the Greek words 'episteme' and 'logos'. 'Episteme' can be translated as
'knowledge' or 'understanding' or 'acquaintance', while 'logos' can be
translated as 'account' or 'argument' or 'reason'. … Although the term
'epistemology' is no more than a couple of centuries old, the field of
epistemology is at least as old as any in philosophy." The phrase "epistemic
status" does not occur in the entry as retrieved.

**CNRTL / TLFi.** *épistémologie*: "1906 « critique des sciences » (Nouv. Lar.
ill. Suppl.). Composé du gr. ἐπιστήμη « science » et de -logie"; "souvent
entendu dans le sens de Philosophie des sciences".

**Treccani.** *epistemologia*: from English *epistemology*; in nineteenth-century
philosophy "la parte della gnoseologia che più in particolare si occupava dei
metodi e dei fondamenti della conoscenza scientifica", now commonly a synonym of
philosophy of science. *gnoseologia*: "dal lat. mod. gnoseologia, termine
coniato dal filosofo ted. A. G. Baumgarten (1714-1762)".

**DWDS.** *Erkenntnistheorie*: "Gebiet der Philosophie, das sich mit den
Bedingungen für Erkenntnisse und Wissen befasst"; see also *Epistemologie*.
*epistemisch* is listed with *epistemologisch* and *erkenntnistheoretisch* as
related expressions (OpenThesaurus), without a definition of its own.

So, as far as these entries go: English *epistemology* and German
*Erkenntnistheorie* name the theory of knowledge, while French *épistémologie*
and Italian *epistemologia* lean towards the philosophy of science, with
*gnoseologia* for the general theory. This is a reading of four dictionary
entries, not a history of the words.

**W3C PROV-DM.** "Provenance is information about entities, activities, and
people involved in producing a piece of data or thing, which can be used to form
assessments about its quality, reliability or trustworthiness." An entity is "a
physical, digital, conceptual, or other kind of thing with some fixed aspects";
an activity "something that occurs over a period of time and acts upon or with
entities"; an agent "something that bears some form of responsibility for an
activity taking place, for the existence of an entity, or for another agent's
activity". "A derivation is a transformation of an entity into another, an
update of an entity resulting in a new one, or the construction of a new entity
based on a pre-existing entity." "Attribution is the ascribing of an entity to
an agent." Provenance, so defined, is something assessments *can be formed
from*; the model does not itself say that what an entity states is true or
justified.

**Meyer, "Applying Design by Contract".** Assertions are preconditions and
postconditions of individual routines and class invariants; "a missing
precondition clause is equivalent to the clause Require True". The paper names
"the research on program proving and systematic program construction pioneered
by Floyd, Hoare, and Dijkstra" as a source. In this sense a contract is a
checkable condition on a routine. The class `lisp-critic-contract` records a
description whose policy slots no code reads; it is not a contract in this
sense.

**Fischer 1987.** Abstract: the LISP-CRITIC "has knowledge about how to improve
LISP programs locally, following a style as defined by its rules. The advice
given is based on the hypothesized knowledge of the user contained in the
system's model of the user. Additional tools (e.g., a knowledge browser and
visualization support) are available to explain and illustrate the advice."
Section 4: suggestions "can make the code either more cognitively efficient
(e.g., more readable and concise) or more machine efficient (e.g., smaller and
faster)"; "the user can inspect the modifications and accept or deny them. On
demand, the system explains and justifies its suggestions"; "The user model …
obtains information from the rules that have fired, from the statistical
analyzer and from the knowledge structures that have been visited. In return,
it determines which rules should fire and what kind of explanations should be
generated"; KAESTLE is used "to illustrate the functioning and validity of
certain rules"; "The critic operates only on the code; the system does not have
any knowledge of specific application areas or algorithms". Acknowledgements:
Boecker "developed many of the original ideas, the set of rules and the
interpreter for it"; Lemke "contributed to the general framework for incremental
learning"; Nieper "developed KAESTLE"; Morel "implemented the explanation
capabilities"; Burns "implemented the statistical analysis package"; Cormack
"analyzed user programs with the statistical package". The OCR text has
recognition errors ("if'Tllrove" for "improve"); quotations are normalized.

Nothing in the paper, or in the engine source read for [rule-base.md](rule-base.md),
connects Fischer's system with Riesbeck's.

**SEP, "Argument and Argumentation".** "Stephen Toulmin's 1958 book The Uses of
Argument has been tremendously influential in a wide range of fields, including
critical thinking education, rhetoric, speech communication, and computer
science". Its aim "was to criticize the assumption … that any significant
argument can be formulated in purely formal, deductive terms". The passages read
do not describe the parts of Toulmin's model.

**Seidl, iX 13/2026 (abstract).** "Wer generiert, darf nicht prüfen: Um diesen
alten Grundsatz kreisen die aktuellen Diskussionen im Software-Testing. Code und
Tests zu erzeugen, ist billig geworden; das Urteil darüber nicht." And: "Lässt ein
Team Code und Tests im selben Kontext erzeugen, können die Tests auf Bestehen
statt auf fachliche Korrektheit optimiert sein."

## How the disciplines use the words

Read across the sources above. Where a source was not read, the row gives only
what the assignment names, and says so.

| Field | Word | What it is about | Basis here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Epistemology | *epistemology*, *epistemic* | knowledge, understanding, the account that makes belief knowledge (Plato's "true opinion accompanied by reason"); justification and evidence | SEP Steup and Neta; *Theaetetus* 201c; Ferrier 1854 |
| Conversation analysis | *epistemic status* vs *epistemic stance* | the assignment names Heritage's distinction between the two | **not read** |
| Linguistics | *evidentiality* vs *epistemic modality* | the assignment names Aikhenvald's distinction: how a speaker came to know vs how sure the speaker is | **not read** |
| Argumentation | *claim*, *data/grounds*, *warrant* | the assignment names Toulmin's three parts; SEP confirms the book's role, not its terms | SEP "Argument and Argumentation"; terms **not read** |
| Information systems | *provenance*, *derivation*, *attribution* | entities, activities and agents involved in producing a thing; "used to form assessments" | W3C PROV-DM |
| Software engineering | *contract* | checkable preconditions, postconditions, invariants | Meyer |
| Lexicography | *épistémologie*, *epistemologia*, *gnoseologia*, *Erkenntnistheorie* | French and Italian lean to philosophy of science; *gnoseologia* and *Erkenntnistheorie* to the general theory of knowledge | CNRTL, Treccani, DWDS |

What follows from the rows that were read:

1. *Epistemic* concerns knowing and its grounds. It does not by itself say whose
   knowing, of what, or on which grounds.
2. Provenance, in PROV's sense, is about production — who and what made a thing
   — and is an input to assessment, not an assessment.
3. If the named distinctions hold as the assignment names them, three of the
   fields keep apart what HyperDoc's labels sometimes run together: how a record
   came to be held (evidentiality, as named), how sure or how authorised anyone is
   (modality, stance, assessment), and what licenses going from grounds to a claim
   (warrant, as named).

## Operational definitions (proposed)

What is classified is a **record**: a statement HyperDoc holds as data, with its
targets, its author or producing procedure, its time and the source state it
concerns. Not the proposition, and not the world.

| Mode | A record has this mode when | It needs | It never becomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| observed | it restates what a named probe or reading returned from a named source at a named state | the source state and the procedure; anyone can repeat it | a statement about the event the source reports. A report that a test passed is observed as a report; the passing is *reported*, not observed |
| derived | a named, deterministic rule produced it from recorded inputs | the rule and the inputs | stronger than its inputs |
| inferred | a named author concluded it from recorded observations or derivations, without a rule that produces it | author and basis (a resolvable list of records) | an observation, however often repeated |
| hypothesized | it is put forward to be tested | what would test it | evidence for itself |

- The four are **mutually exclusive per record** and **not per proposition**: one
  proposition can have an inferred record and, later, an observed one. Later
  evidence adds a record; it does not change the old record's mode.
- *Derived* or *inferred*: a statement is derived exactly when a rule that anyone
  can run again produces it from recorded inputs. If the step needs an author's
  judgement, it is inferred.
- Four further properties are **dimensions, not further values**:
  - attribution and provenance — who or what produced the record, from what
    (PROV);
  - source state — content identity (digest) and, separately, revision membership;
  - verification — which property of an artifact was checked, how, by whom
    (`:artifact-verified` in PR #43 is a derived record about an artifact);
  - assessment — a decision by a named authority under named criteria, such as a
    review or an acceptance.
- Two of PR #43's warrants fit this scheme as modes: `:observed-in-source` is
  observed; `:artifact-verified` is derived. `:reported` is observed *of a report*
  together with an attribution of the reported event to that report.
  `:specified-context` is neither: it is a parameter stipulated by whoever
  specified the reading, and belongs with the source state, not with the modes.

## Recommendation

**Retain `epistemic status` and restrict its meaning.** Use it, in code and prose,
only for the mode in which a record holds its statement, with the four values
defined above, preferably in the self-describing spelling of
`upstream-assessment` (`:source-observed`, `:mechanically-derived`,
`:design-inference`, `:working-hypothesis`). Do not use it for attribution,
source state, verification or assessment; give each its own field. In German
prose, *Begründungsweise* names the same thing; *Herkunft* names attribution and
provenance and should not stand in for it. Before any further module introduces
its own value set, the existing ones listed above should be reconciled; that is
an implementation decision and is not made here.
